# Existing Scholarship on Atrocity Economics

This document surveys prior work on the economic aspects of state violence, military spending, and taxpayer complicity. Our book builds on this foundation while offering novel data analysis and synthesis.

---

## Academic Books

### Anderton & Brauer (2016): Economic Aspects of Genocides, Other Mass Atrocities, and Their Prevention
- **Publisher**: Oxford University Press
- **Significance**: The definitive academic treatment
- **Key findings**:
  - Assembled 200+ atrocity episodes from 1900-2014
  - 80-250 million civilian deaths from genocide/mass killing in 20th century
  - Atrocities far exceed war deaths
  - Economic framework for understanding perpetrator, victim, and bystander behavior
- **Methodological insights**:
  - Cost-constraint analysis of genocide
  - Microeconomic incentives facing perpetrators
  - Macroeconomic consequences for affected countries
- **Gaps we can fill**: They focus on perpetrator economics; we focus on taxpayer funding

### Rummel, R.J.: Death by Government (1994) and Statistics of Democide (1997)
- **Publisher**: Transaction Publishers
- **Significance**: Created the concept of "democide" and compiled definitive 20th century data
- **Key findings**:
  - 262 million killed by governments in 20th century
  - Soviet Union: ~62 million
  - Communist China: ~35 million
  - Nazi Germany: ~21 million
  - Power kills; absolute power kills absolutely
- **Data availability**: Full tables at hawaii.edu/powerkills
- **Critiques**: Communist figures contested; methodology transparent but subjective
- **Our use**: Primary historical data source for 20th century democide

### Bank, Stark & Thorndike (2008): War and Taxes
- **Publisher**: Urban Institute Press
- **Significance**: Historical analysis of how America pays for wars
- **Key findings**:
  - Revolutionary War through Iraq War tax analysis
  - WWII: Sacrifice through taxation (94% top rate)
  - Post-9/11: Tax cuts during war (unprecedented)
  - "Resistance and reluctance are recurring themes"
- **Our use**: Historical context for American tax/war relationship

### Kreps, Sarah: Taxing Wars
- **Significance**: Democratic accountability through war taxation
- **Key argument**: War taxes held leaders accountable; their absence removes democratic check
- **Our use**: Theoretical framework for taxpayer responsibility

## Research Datasets

### Uppsala Conflict Data Program (UCDP)
- **Institution**: Uppsala University
- **Coverage**: 1946-present
- **Significance**: Global standard for conflict data
- **Key datasets**:
  - Armed Conflict Dataset: conflicts with 25+ battle deaths/year
  - GED: Georeferenced events (individual incidents)
  - One-Sided Violence: government killing of civilians
  - Battle Deaths: Annual dyad-level aggregates
- **Methodology**: Event-based, multi-source verification
- **Our use**: Primary modern conflict data

### Correlates of War (COW)
- **Institution**: University of Michigan (founded 1963)
- **Coverage**: 1816-present
- **Significance**: Longest-running war data project
- **Key datasets**:
  - Wars (inter-state, intra-state, extra-state, non-state)
  - Militarized Interstate Disputes
  - National Material Capabilities (includes milex)
- **Our use**: Historical war data, military capability indicators

### Political Terror Scale (PTS)
- **Institution**: UNC Asheville
- **Coverage**: 1976-present
- **Significance**: Annual human rights measurement
- **Methodology**: Code government behavior 1-5 from Amnesty, State Dept, HRW reports
- **Our use**: Correlate repression with spending

### SIPRI Military Expenditure Database
- **Institution**: Stockholm International Peace Research Institute
- **Coverage**: 1949-present
- **Significance**: Authoritative military spending data
- **Methodology**: Standardized definitions across countries
- **Our use**: Primary military spending source

### Maddison Project
- **Institution**: University of Groningen
- **Coverage**: 1 CE - present (sparse early, dense post-1800)
- **Significance**: Historical GDP estimates
- **Our use**: Historical income for taxpayer calculations

## Key Academic Papers

### Lacina & Gleditsch (2005): "Monitoring Trends in Global Combat"
- Methodology for consistent battle death counting
- Basis for UCDP approach

### Obermeyer et al. (2008): "Fifty Years of Violent War Deaths"
- Critique of existing death estimates
- Statistical approaches to uncertainty

### Burnham et al. (2006): "Mortality after the 2003 invasion of Iraq"
- Lancet study estimating 655,000 excess deaths
- Controversial but methodologically rigorous

### Verwimp (2006): "Machetes and Firearms: The Organization of Massacres in Rwanda"
- Microeconomic analysis of genocide
- Weapon choice and killing efficiency
- **Key insight**: Machetes used by poor against poor; firearms for organized massacres

### Economic Effects of Genocide: Evidence from Rwanda
- Synthetic control method
- 58% GDP decline in 1994
- Recovery took 17 years to close gap

## Journalism and Reports

### Brown University Costs of War Project
- **URL**: watson.brown.edu/costsofwar
- **Significance**: Comprehensive post-9/11 accounting
- **Key findings**:
  - 940,000+ direct deaths
  - 3.6-3.7 million indirect deaths
  - $8+ trillion cost
  - 38+ million displaced
- **Our use**: Primary source for post-9/11 analysis

### Bureau of Investigative Journalism: Drone War
- **URL**: thebureauinvestigates.com/projects/drone-war
- **Significance**: Most comprehensive drone strike database
- **Coverage**: Pakistan, Yemen, Somalia, Afghanistan
- **Our use**: Drone warfare casualty data

### National Priorities Project
- **URL**: nationalpriorities.org
- **Significance**: Taxpayer cost breakdowns
- **Key figure**: $5,109/taxpayer for militarism (2023)
- **Our use**: Contemporary taxpayer calculations

### The Intercept: The Drone Papers
- **Significance**: Leaked documents on drone targeting
- **Key insight**: 90% of deaths were not intended targets in some periods

## Gaps in Existing Literature

Our book fills several gaps:

1. **Taxpayer perspective**: Most work focuses on perpetrators, victims, or states—not ordinary citizens funding violence through taxes

2. **Cross-era comparison**: No single work systematically compares taxpayer complicity across different countries and eras

3. **Rich vs. poor violence economics**: Limited analysis of how national wealth affects the economics of atrocity

4. **American lifetime perspective**: No work traces a single cohort's tax contributions to violence over 50 years

5. **Data integration**: Existing datasets are siloed; we integrate across sources

6. **Accessible presentation**: Academic work is inaccessible; we aim for general readers

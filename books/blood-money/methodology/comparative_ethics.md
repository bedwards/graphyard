# Methodology: Comparative Ethics

This document outlines how we present multiple ethical frameworks for evaluating taxpayer responsibility for state violence.

---

## The Core Ethical Question

When a government uses military force that kills people, what moral responsibility do the citizens who fund that government bear?

This question has no single answer. Different ethical traditions reach different conclusions. Our book presents the data through multiple lenses, allowing readers to apply their own moral framework.

---

## Three Major Ethical Traditions

### 1. Just War Theory

**Core premise**: Some wars are morally justified; killing enemy combatants in a just war is permissible.

**Key principles**:
- *Jus ad bellum* (justice of war): Was there legitimate cause?
- *Jus in bello* (justice in war): Was conduct proportionate and discriminating?
- Combatants are legitimate targets; civilians are not
- Collateral damage is permissible if proportionate and unintended

**Taxpayer implications**:
- Funding a just war is not morally problematic
- Funding violations of jus in bello (torture, civilian targeting) implicates taxpayers
- Complicity is attenuated: soldiers bear primary responsibility
- Democratic accountability: voters can change policy

**What to count**: Only unjust killings—violations of proportionality, targeting civilians, wars of aggression

**Major thinkers**: Augustine, Aquinas, Grotius, Walzer

### 2. Absolute Pacifism

**Core premise**: All violence is wrong; there are no exceptions.

**Key principles**:
- Killing is always impermissible regardless of context
- Self-defense does not justify lethal force
- The state has no special license to kill
- Ends never justify violent means

**Taxpayer implications**:
- All military spending implicates taxpayers in violence
- There is no such thing as "justified" military killing
- Even funding defense is morally problematic
- Tax resistance is a moral imperative

**What to count**: All deaths caused by military action, including enemy combatants

**Major thinkers**: Tolstoy, Gandhi, Martin Luther King Jr. (in part), Quaker tradition

### 3. Contingent/Pragmatic Pacifism

**Core premise**: War is almost never justified in practice, even if theoretically possible.

**Key principles**:
- Just war criteria are rarely if ever met in reality
- The logic of war tends toward escalation and atrocity
- Non-violent alternatives are usually available and more effective
- Wars fought for "just" reasons still produce unjust outcomes

**Taxpayer implications**:
- Most military spending funds unjust violence
- Even "defensive" wars rarely stay defensive
- Taxpayers should presume military action is unjust unless proven otherwise
- Reform, not resistance, may be more effective

**What to count**: All deaths, with special attention to whether just war criteria were met

**Major thinkers**: Larry May, David Cortright, many Vietnam-era critics

## Secondary Ethical Considerations

### Democratic vs. Authoritarian Responsibility

Are citizens of democracies more responsible than subjects of dictatorships?

**Arguments for greater democratic responsibility**:
- Citizens can vote, protest, organize
- Government claims to act on citizens' behalf
- Free press means citizens can know what's done in their name

**Arguments for equal responsibility**:
- Individual citizens have minimal power even in democracies
- Manufactured consent and propaganda limit true choice
- Non-voters didn't choose the government either

**Arguments for lesser democratic responsibility**:
- Minority voters opposed the war
- True alternatives may not be available
- System is designed to limit citizen power

**Our approach**: Present both views; let readers decide.

### Individual vs. Collective Responsibility

Is responsibility individual (each taxpayer bears a share) or collective (the nation as a whole)?

**Individual perspective**:
- Each taxpayer can calculate their contribution
- Personal moral accounting is possible
- Individual action (resistance) is meaningful

**Collective perspective**:
- Responsibility is diffused and shared
- Structural change matters more than individual action
- National reckoning is more important than personal guilt

**Our approach**: Calculate individual shares while acknowledging collective dimension.

### Intentionality and Knowledge

Does it matter what the taxpayer knew or intended?

**Ignorance arguments**:
- Many citizens don't know their taxes fund violence
- Government secrecy limits knowledge
- Complexity makes understanding impossible

**Counter-arguments**:
- Ignorance is often willful
- Information is available to those who seek it
- Ignorance doesn't eliminate causal contribution

**Our approach**: Note information availability in each era; acknowledge varying access.

## Comparing Across Contexts

### The Normalization Problem

How do we compare taxpayers in different contexts?
- A Nazi German paying RM 500/year in 1942 vs. an American paying $3,000 in 2003
- Both funded violence, but economies, scales, and choices differed

**Possible normalizations**:
1. **Raw contribution**: Absolute tax paid toward military
2. **Wealth-normalized**: Tax as percentage of income
3. **Per-death**: Tax contribution divided by deaths caused
4. **Per-victim weighted by intentionality**: Account for targeting vs. collateral
5. **GDP-normalized**: Military spending as share of national economy

**Our approach**: Present multiple normalizations; each reveals different patterns.

### The Technology Problem

Is high-tech killing different from low-tech killing?

**Arguments for moral equivalence**:
- Dead is dead regardless of method
- Results matter more than means
- Technology doesn't change moral status

**Arguments for moral difference**:
- Precision weapons reduce collateral damage
- Low-tech mass killing (machetes) is more visceral
- Technology enables killing at scale

**Our approach**: Present data on both; note that technology affects efficiency but not culpability.

### The Direct/Indirect Problem

How do we weight direct killing vs. deaths from sanctions, neglect, or policy?

**Direct deaths**: Bombs, bullets, executed prisoners

**Indirect deaths**:
- Sanctions causing medicine shortages
- Refugee policy causing deaths in flight
- Infrastructure destruction causing disease
- Long-term trauma causing suicide

**Arguments for counting indirect deaths**:
- Foreseeable consequences are still consequences
- Policy choices have known outcomes
- The dead are equally dead

**Arguments for discounting indirect deaths**:
- Causation is more attenuated
- Other factors intervene
- Intent differs fundamentally

**Our approach**: Calculate both; present conservative (direct only) and inclusive (all) figures.

## Moral Distance Framework

We propose a "layers of responsibility" framework:

```
Layer 1: Direct perpetrator (soldier firing weapon)
    ↓ Most responsible
Layer 2: Commander ordering action
    ↓
Layer 3: Political leadership authorizing policy
    ↓
Layer 4: Citizens who elected leadership (democracies)
    ↓
Layer 5: Taxpayers who funded the action
    ↓ Least direct, but still connected
```

Each layer represents attenuation of direct responsibility, but not elimination. The taxpayer at Layer 5 is the most removed, but their money enabled all the layers above.

## What This Book Does NOT Do

We do not:
- Assign guilt or innocence to individual taxpayers
- Declare one ethical framework correct
- Equate all forms of violence as morally identical
- Ignore context, history, and alternatives
- Claim perfect data or certain conclusions

We do:
- Present data that each framework can interpret
- Make calculations transparent and reproducible
- Acknowledge uncertainty and limitations
- Let readers apply their own values to the numbers

---

## Applying Frameworks to Data

For each major case study, we calculate:

| Metric | Just War | Pacifist | Contingent |
|--------|----------|----------|------------|
| Deaths counted | Unjust civilian only | All deaths | All deaths + justice assessment |
| Taxpayer share | Only for violations | For all military | For all + premium for unjust |
| Moral weight | Context-dependent | Equal | Graduated by justice |

This allows readers to see the data through their preferred lens.

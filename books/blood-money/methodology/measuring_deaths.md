# Methodology: Measuring Deaths

## The Central Problem

Counting deaths from state violence is harder than it sounds. Different sources use different definitions, methodologies, and scopes. This document explains how we approach the problem.

---

## Key Distinctions

### 1. Direct vs. Indirect Deaths

**Direct deaths** result from violence itself:
- Battle deaths (combatants killed in fighting)
- Execution and murder (deliberate killing outside combat)
- Collateral damage (civilians killed in military operations)

**Indirect deaths** result from conditions caused by violence:
- Disease (healthcare system collapse, epidemics)
- Starvation (food system disruption, blockades, sanctions)
- Displacement (deaths during flight, in refugee camps)
- Long-term trauma (suicides, reduced life expectancy)

**Our approach**: We calculate both. For most analyses, we present two numbers:
- Conservative: Direct deaths only
- Inclusive: Direct + indirect deaths

The ratio of indirect to direct deaths varies by conflict:
- Modern conflicts: ~4:1 (per Brown University Costs of War)
- Historical famines: Can be 10:1 or higher
- Rapid genocides: Closer to 1:1

### 2. Combatant vs. Civilian Deaths

**Combatants** are uniformed members of organized armed forces.
**Civilians** are everyone else.

The distinction matters for just war theory, which permits killing enemy combatants under certain conditions while prohibiting targeting civilians.

**Gray areas**:
- Irregular forces (guerrillas, militias, resistance fighters)
- Dual-status individuals (farmer by day, fighter by night)
- Forced conscripts
- Child soldiers

**Our approach**: We track these separately where data allows. Some analyses include only civilian deaths; others include all deaths.

### 3. Intentional vs. Incidental Deaths

**Intentional**: Deaths that were the purpose of the action
- Genocide targets
- Execution victims
- Terrorist attack victims

**Incidental (collateral)**: Deaths that were foreseeable but not intended
- Civilians near military targets
- Hospital patients after infrastructure destruction
- Famine victims from economic disruption

**Negligent**: Deaths from reckless disregard
- Using weapons known to cause disproportionate civilian harm
- Failing to verify targets
- Ignoring humanitarian corridors

**Our approach**: Intentionality affects moral analysis but not counting. A dead child is dead regardless of intent.

### 4. State vs. Non-State Violence

**State violence**: Perpetrated by governments or their agents
- Military operations
- Police actions
- Intelligence operations
- Paramilitaries under state direction

**Non-state violence**: Perpetrated by non-governmental groups
- Terrorist attacks
- Criminal violence
- Independent militia actions

**Our approach**: We focus primarily on state violence because that's what taxes fund. But we include non-state violence when states fund, train, or direct the perpetrators (proxy forces).

## Data Sources

### Primary Sources

| Source | Coverage | Definition | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|--------|----------|------------|-----------|------------|
| **UCDP** | 1946-present | Armed conflict (≥25 battle deaths/year) | Systematic, rigorous, georeferenced | Excludes one-sided violence in peace |
| **Rummel Democide** | 1900-1999 | Death by government | Comprehensive historical coverage | Author estimates, not updated |
| **Political Terror Scale** | 1976-present | State repression (1-5 scale) | Annual, multiple sources | Not death counts |
| **Iraq Body Count** | 2003-present | Documented civilian deaths | Incident-level detail | Iraq only, conservative |
| **Costs of War** | 2001-present | US post-9/11 wars | Comprehensive, includes indirect | US conflicts only |
| **ACLED** | 1997-present | Political violence events | Global, current | Event-based, not war-focused |

### Secondary Sources

- Academic studies of specific conflicts
- Truth commission reports
- Human rights organization reports
- Declassified intelligence documents
- Journalistic investigations

## Attribution Methods

### Assigning Deaths to Countries

For each death, we must attribute responsibility. This is straightforward for some cases (German forces killing Holocaust victims) and contested for others (famine deaths under British rule in India).

**Our attribution framework**:

1. **Direct responsibility**: The state whose forces committed the killing
2. **Command responsibility**: The state that ordered or authorized the action
3. **Funding responsibility**: States that provided weapons, training, or funds
4. **Enabling responsibility**: States that provided diplomatic cover or intelligence

For calculations in this book, we focus on (1) and (2), with (3) noted where significant.

### Temporal Attribution

When deaths should be counted for budget calculations:
- **Year of death**: Deaths count in the year they occur
- **Year of action**: Deaths count when the causal action occurred
- **Averaged**: Deaths spread across conflict duration

We use **year of death** as the default, with sensitivity analysis for long-latency deaths (e.g., Agent Orange victims dying decades later).

---

## Uncertainty and Ranges

Death counts are never exact. We present:
- **Low estimate**: Most conservative credible figure
- **Mid estimate**: Central estimate or median of range
- **High estimate**: Upper bound of credible range

When sources disagree:
- If methodologies differ, we note both with explanations
- If sources conflict on facts, we use the more credible source
- If genuinely uncertain, we widen the range

## Specific Methodologies by Conflict Type

### Genocide
Use academic consensus where available. Major genocides (Holocaust, Rwanda, Armenian) have extensive scholarship. Ranges still exist.

### Colonial Violence
Highly contested. We rely on demographic analysis (population decline) rather than event counting. Include famines when policy-induced.

### Modern Warfare
UCDP for battle deaths. Iraq Body Count methodology for Iraq. Bureau of Investigative Journalism for drone strikes.

### Covert Operations
Necessarily incomplete. Use declassified documents, truth commission reports, academic estimates. Note high uncertainty.

### Indirect Deaths
Apply ratios from peer-reviewed epidemiological studies. Note that these are estimates, not counts.

---

## Known Limitations

1. **Undercounting**: We almost certainly undercount. Many deaths go unrecorded.

2. **Recording bias**: Deaths in media-saturated conflicts are better documented than remote violence.

3. **Political distortion**: Governments undercount their victims; opponents may overcount.

4. **Definitional ambiguity**: Reasonable people disagree on what counts.

5. **Attribution difficulty**: Many deaths have multiple causes; assignment to one actor is artificial.

We proceed despite these limitations because:
- Imperfect data is better than no data
- Ranges capture uncertainty
- Consistent methodology enables comparison
- Transparency about methods invites correction
